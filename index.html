What We Observe

Six favourite targets, from the easy to the challenging.

Beginner

The Moon

Even binoculars show craters and mountains along the edge of the shadow.

Best seen: any clear night

Beginner

Jupiter

A small telescope shows its four largest moons as tiny dots in a line.

Best seen: when it is high in the sky

Beginner

Saturn

The rings are clearly visible even in a modest telescope. Everyone remembers their first look.

Best seen: around opposition

Intermediate

Mars

Surface detail only shows up when Mars is close to Earth, which happens roughly every 26 months.

Best seen: near opposition

Intermediate

Andromeda Galaxy

About 2.5 million light-years away, and still visible to the eye from a dark site.

Best seen: autumn evenings

Intermediate

Orion Nebula

A glowing cloud where new stars are forming, found in the sword of Orion.

Best seen: winter evenings

New to stargazing?

You do not need expensive equipment. Start here.

How to begin

  1. Learn the main constellations with a free sky map.
  2. Let your eyes adjust to the dark for 20 minutes.
  3. Try a pair of binoculars before buying a telescope.
  4. Come to one of our beginner nights.

What to bring

  • A warm jacket
  • A red-light torch
  • A flask of something hot
  • Patience and curiosity
